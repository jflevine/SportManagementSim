      The Offer: instructor guide

      Twelve minutes, pods of 4–6, one device per pod. Pods negotiate a fictional private-equity-backed offer for a nonprofit Philadelphia youth soccer club, live through a resale shock, and defend the tradeoff in a four-sentence board statement. There is no winning score.

      All figures are classroom assumptions. Riverwards United SC and Keystone Play Holdings are fictional. The model is simplified and is not a forecast or a description of any real deal.

      Debrief questions

      1. Who decides access now? Under a full sale with market pricing, the owner sets fees and hours; the board’s waiver policy goes with the club. Under a partnership, the club keeps its waiver policy but rents prime time on someone else’s terms.
      2. What does the club give up for capital? Control of price, of video and data, and of who sits at the table. Ask pods to name the dollar figure their protections cost (the Year 5 screen shows it).
      3. Which protection survives a resale? In this model, contract terms (a fee cap, a binding seat, a buy-back right) travel with the deal; a non-binding advisory council does not bind anyone. The fee cap still expires after Year 5. A buy-back right is only as good as the trigger it is tied to and the money to exercise it.
      4. Where does governance sit: NFHS, the state association, or none? NFHS and state associations such as PIAA govern member high school athletics. A private club’s ownership deal, prices, and streaming rights sit outside that structure. In this scenario the levers are the contract, the city (which controls field permits and the grant), and whatever national governing body affiliation the club keeps. Push students to say “mostly none” and then ask who should fill the gap.

      Misconceptions to correct

      • “Private equity is the villain” or “private equity is the rescue.” The model gives private capital real benefits: more players and a year-round facility. The question is who governs the asset after the money arrives.
      • “Nonprofit means cheap and open.” Declining still raises the fee to $340, keeps a waitlist, and depends on a city budget the club does not control.
      • “An advisory council is governance.” Non-binding advice moves accountability from Low to Low–Medium at best.
      • “A fee cap is permanent.” The cap here runs five years. The resale shock shows why the end date matters.
      • “More players means more access.” Compare the low-income share, not just total players. A bigger club can serve fewer low-income kids as a share, or more as a count.
      • “High school rules cover club sport.” NFHS and PIAA do not regulate a private club’s prices or ownership.
      • “These numbers are real.” They are classroom assumptions chosen to make the tradeoffs visible.

      Year 3 shock: for S1/S2 the fund sells its portfolio and the new owner raises prices 25%. Without a cap, the fee rises 25% and the low-income share drops 4 points. With a cap, the cap holds through Year 5. With a cap and T3c, the board may trigger its buy-back right; with T3c but no cap, the trigger never fires (a useful debrief point). For S3, a city budget delay closes both fields for half the spring and players drop to 880 that season.

      The per-game streaming price shown for T2a is illustrative. Per-game paywalls for youth events have been reported in the press; if you cite one in class, use a current, credible source and name the company only when that source does.
