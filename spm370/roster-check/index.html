Roster Check (v2)

Instructor fallback / 6 minutes / chapter scenarios with labeled classroom variations

Students vote A, B, or C. Ask for one legal reason before revealing the answer. No individual devices are needed.

1. Luna’s contract

Luna’s contract with ApexForge calls her an independent contractor. Managers now set her daily start times and review her stream scripts. What can you conclude?

  1. The contract settles her status.
  2. Any control automatically makes her an employee.
  3. Choose the governing law and examine the full relationship.

C. A label or a single control fact does not settle status. For Title VII, the common-law inquiry considers the whole relationship: control, payment, equipment, duration, benefits, taxes, and more. Darden decided ERISA; Faush applies common-law principles to Title VII in the Third Circuit.

2. Which law?

Classroom variation: Luna also asks whether she is owed federal overtime pay. Which classification inquiry is the starting point?

  1. The FLSA’s economic-realities inquiry.
  2. Title VII’s discrimination framework.
  3. California’s ABC test for every U.S. worker.

A. Federal overtime raises the FLSA’s economic-realities inquiry. Worker status is only one step: statutory coverage, hours worked, and exemptions also matter. California’s ABC test applies only for specified California purposes.

3. Audience appeal

A hiring director rejects a qualified woman for a producer role because “our audience expects a man.” Which theory best fits?

  1. Disparate impact, because every decision has an impact.
  2. Disparate treatment because of sex.
  3. No possible claim if fans really prefer men.

B. The stated reason is a sex-based decision. Assumed fan preference ordinarily cannot justify exclusion, and the BFOQ exception is narrow. Coverage and evidence are still required.

4. GlobalArena’s rule

GlobalArena requires analysts, coaches, and content staff to live near headquarters. Applicant data show a national-origin disparity, and an earlier hybrid pilot worked for remote-capable jobs. What should you investigate?

  1. Nothing, because the rule uses identical words for everyone.
  2. Whether people who live far away are a separate Title VII protected class.
  3. Disparate impact, job relatedness, business necessity, and the alternative.

C. A specific neutral practice can raise a disparate-impact claim. Analyze the causal disparity, the employer’s job-related justification, and an effective less discriminatory alternative. Residence alone is not a protected characteristic.

5. Orion Forge’s explanation

Orion Forge says its new head of production “fits our culture.” Which request best helps test that explanation?

  1. Interview scores, written criteria, and decision-maker messages.
  2. The organization’s follower count.
  3. Nothing; “culture fit” is lawful by definition.

A. Those records test whether the reason was defined in advance, applied consistently, and actually drove the decision. Subjective language is not automatically unlawful, but it cannot conceal a protected-trait decision.

6. A proven violation

Suppose a court finds that Orion Forge (35 employees) discriminated in a way that cost an employee wages. Which statement is most accurate?

  1. The $300,000 cap limits every kind of Title VII relief.
  2. Back pay may address lost wages; the combined compensatory and punitive cap does not include back pay or front pay.
  3. A proven violation means an automatic $300,000 award.

B. Relief follows the proven injury. For an employer with 15–100 employees, the combined cap on compensatory and punitive damages is $50,000. Back pay and front pay fall outside that cap, and neither is automatic.

Debrief

Which answer changed after you identified the governing law? Which answer needed more facts? Revisit those before Thursday.
